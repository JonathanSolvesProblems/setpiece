# What Setpiece does, in full

The short version is in the [README](../README.md). This is the long one: each thing the app does, how, and what was measured.

### The set designer

- **Say the shot.** Pinch Talk and say what happens: "a night chase, a red car racing past a burning truck, two actors running at camera, a dragon on a rooftop". The set clears and the pieces drop onto the slate one after another, as the designer writes them (measured from a desktop: the first piece a median of 1.3 s after the request, the whole set 2.8 s; on the headset, two sentences in my own words put the first piece down 1.6 s after I stopped speaking and the whole set at 3.3 and 3.7 s, the second on the live site), with the actors posed and dressed, the vehicles painted, and the camera stood, raised and aimed for the shot with a lens on it. The panel says what was built and one thing to try next. The designer also chooses the set's light from the sentence (a night chase is night, a duel at sunset golden hour, a diner booth studio, a product shot one pool of light on a dark stage), which colours the key light and the fill and paints the sky behind every frame on the board and around you when you step inside: a sun or a moon where the key light stands, clouds lit by it, stars and a far city with its windows lit at night, hills and haze by day. The sky is a picture painted when the light changes (`src/sky-paint.ts`), so it costs the headset nothing to show; five sentences through the real model on Nov 14 each got the light they named. A wearer the listener does not hear pinches Try a shot on the panel for one of three shots. In a session with no microphone allowed (the headset cannot ask for it mid-session), Talk reads Next shot instead and builds the next of those three, and the designer says why.
- **It answers aloud.** As the pieces drop, the designer says what it is building ("Two women argue across a kitchen counter while a man watches from the doorway"); when it looks through the lens and moves the camera, it says what it changed; in Cover it, it calls each shot. Its line is read by ElevenLabs, in her voice by default, his, or none from the SPOKEN row on the panel's Stage tab. ElevenLabs is sent only the designer's reply: never your voice, and never the transcript of what you said (the reply describes the set you asked for, so it can name what you named). The voice reads nothing else: the server signs each line as the designer writes it, and words that arrive without that signature are refused. While it is talking, Keep listening does not reopen the microphone, so it is not heard as your next sentence, and a press on Talk stops it mid-line. Without a key, or with the account's allowance spent, it stays quiet and the panel says it all.
- **It talks a newcomer through.** The call sheet's steps are also said aloud, once each, by the same voice: where to point and pinch (only if the set has not landed by itself after a moment), that the next sentence is yours, how to move things and snap, what Step inside does, that the board is full. Those lines never change, so they were recorded ahead of time in both voices and ship as files (`src/spoken-lines.ts`, `tools/make-voice-lines.mjs`): saying one calls no server. `npm run check:claims` fails if a line's words and its recording differ. While it talks a newcomer through, a small yellow arrow bobs over the thing it is asking for (Talk, the piece to move, Snap frame, Step inside), and a step's line stops the moment the step is done. Step inside takes one pinch when the walkthrough has asked for it, and two at any other time. Skip the walkthrough is on the panel for as long as it is on, and Walkthrough again (More, Stage) starts a first visit over without leaving the session. Someone who has been through it, or skipped it, hears only a welcome back.
- **The first set is the designer's.** The first time a newcomer lands the set, the designer builds a first set by itself (the first of the three shots to try, asked of the real designer, not a saved reply) and says what it built, then says the next one is yours. The table is alive before the panel has been found. Not for someone returning to a set of their own.
- **Cast as written.** Actors come in a man's and a woman's build, as artists' lay figures do: hers has narrower shoulders, a waist, wider hips and hair to the shoulders, which she keeps in every look. The designer casts each actor from the sentence ("two women arguing in a kitchen, and a man watching from the door" builds two women and a man) and mixes them where it does not say. The library's own reader, used when the designer cannot be reached, casts from the same words: a woman, two women, an actress, a man.
- **Cover it.** Say "cover it", or pinch Shoot coverage once a set stands, and the designer plans the shots that cover the scene as it stands (a wide that sets the place, then closer shots on what it is about: a two-shot, a close-up, a low or high angle), and the app shoots them by itself: the camera moves to each, the frame goes onto the board, and the board fills while you watch. Stepped inside, it cuts you from camera to camera instead, so you stand in each shot. Say stop, pinch Stop coverage or pick up a piece to end it. A coverage shot is not looked at through the lens the way a new set's first frame is, so its framing is checked by measure before the camera goes to it: the subject has to fit the frame (an actor from head to waist, anything else whole). The camera steps back along its own line as far as the stage allows, and if that is not enough a wider lens goes on; the first real coverage I put on a board had a car door filling a 35 mm frame and the top of a head on a 135 mm, and `node tools/test-coverage-fit.mjs` keeps those two from coming back. Each frame carries its shot's name on the board. (The real model planned coverages in testing and the app shot them on the live page and in the emulator; not yet on the headset.)
- **It makes what the library does not have.** A rooftop, a castle gate, a stone bridge, a spaceship: the designer builds them from simple turned and cut shapes (boxes, cylinders, cones, domes, roofs) in the library's own bare wood and model enamels, like a set designer's card model. Each one becomes a piece like any other: it can be picked up, turned, sized with two hands, stood on, struck and undone, and it is still there next time.
- **A set dresser checks its work.** The designer counts footprints well when a piece faces you and badly once it is turned: given 16 sentences written after its instructions were settled, on Nov 13 it twice stood actors inside a car parked side-on. So once a reply is in, and before the camera is placed, the app moves anything standing inside something else out by the shortest step, keeps every piece's middle on the stage and its front off the camera's side, and moves an effect, or a dragon on a roof, with the piece it stood on. A truck parked half off the side stays there: that is how a set shows less of a place too big for it. A rider on a horse, a guard in a made gateway and anything you placed yourself are left where they are. A piece the designer makes is held to 5 m across, 3 m front to back and 4 m high and shrunk whole when it was written bigger: on Nov 16, on the headset, it wrote a castle 7 m across and 6 m high that stood over the whole slate, into the panel and through the storyboard. The panel also moves left for anything that hangs past the slate's left edge, and the monitor and the board rise over a tall piece. Graded by rule on Nov 16 as it ships (asked to begin its answer at once, actors cast as men or women, a greeting answered without building, six lights to choose from, hiding places and the most room a made piece may take spelled out), 13 of those 16 sets had nothing to flag as the designer wrote them, and 14 after the set dresser: it made a boat small without being asked, and named a second fence the library does not have. Earlier that day, before those last two instructions, 15 and 16; before the sixth light, 12 and 14; on Nov 15, 13 and 14; earlier that day, before actors had a build, 15 and 16; on Nov 13, 13 and 15. Each sentence is asked once a run, so a set or two moves between runs.
- **It looks through the lens.** Once a new set's camera stands, the designer is shown the camera's own picture, with a report measured from the pieces beside it: how much of the frame each piece covers, which edges cut it, how far it is and what stands in front of what. If the subject is cut off, hidden or framed wrong, it moves the camera and looks once more; the panel says what it changed. Before any camera is placed, the app itself checks, as a camera operator would, that the lens is not inside a piece and nothing stands between it and the subject, and takes the nearest clear spot when it does.
- **Then say what to change.** "Make the dragon bigger", "add rain", "move the car closer", "low angle on the dragon": the designer reads the set as your hands left it, so a piece you moved stays where you put it, and only what you named changes. "It" is the piece you last touched.
- **What it cannot do.** It can only put down the library's pieces and the shapes it makes; it cannot invent textures, faces, brands or real people, and it is told never to name a brand or a real person. It does not hear audio: the headset turns your words into text itself and only the text is sent, with a description of the set and, for the look through the lens, one small picture from the shot camera.
- **Without it, Setpiece is all still there.** If the designer cannot be reached, the same sentence goes to the library's own reader, which builds what it can from the names it knows and says what it could not place. The hands, the camera, the take and the storyboard never depend on it.

- **The stage is a clapper slate at 1:10** (60 by 40 cm, a 6 by 4 m set). Point at any real surface and a chalk outline of the slate lies on it; pinch and the set lands there. That uses the headset's live hit test, so it needs no room scan. A table the headset has already labelled takes the set by itself. If the headset reports no surface at all, the set floats in front of you, comes with you when you walk away, sit down or turn round, and lands where you touch the table and pinch or where you carry it by its striped front edge.
- **Actors are wooden lay figures; the creature is a tennis ball on a stand** at its eye height. Pick a piece up and move your hand; turn your wrist and it turns. A ring lights under the piece your hand is on. Let go and it stands itself up on the slate. The camera keeps the height you gave it, because a high or a low angle is a choice.
- **Lost the set?** It stays on your real table, so a headset put back on facing elsewhere sees none of it; a yellow arrow near the middle of the view points the way to it, and goes once it is in view. If the headset has set its boundary again and the set came back somewhere else, clap: while the arrow is up, or once the set has stood out of reach for a few seconds and a line offers it, a clap brings it up to be landed where you point. A clap only counts while that offer is on screen, and never while a piece is in a hand, the designer is building, coverage is being shot or a take is running, so a clap for "action" never moves the set.
- **Only the striped edge moves the set.** Hold it and the whole slate follows your hand and turns with your wrist. The slate's top, the monitor and the board do not move it, so a pinch that misses an actor does not carry the set away. If a pinch does catch the edge, a twitch leaves the set where it stood, and a real move is taken back by Undo.
- **Hands or controllers.** Everything a pinch does, a trigger does; the guidance says trigger when controllers are in your hands.
- **The eyeline from each actor to the ball is drawn live** and reported in degrees and in metres on the real set.
- **The camera is a 35 mm film camera.** A director's monitor above the stage shows what its lens sees, at 18, 24, 35, 50, 85 or 135 mm, on a full frame, Super 35, Super 16 or 65 mm format cropped to 16:9. That is four formats. Switch it from tripod to drone and it flies: the legs go, the rotors spin, and you carry it up to 11 m on the real set. Turn your wrist to pan it and tip your wrist to tilt its head on the tripod. Until you tilt it yourself it tilts itself at the nearest actor's eyes. The lens sees the set and nothing else: not the panel, not the card, not your hands.
- **Snap claps the sticks and puts a frame on the storyboard card**: six frames, each with its lens, camera height, distance to the creature and every actor's eyeline written under it, so the card is the shot list and can be read in the headset on its own. **Pinch a frame and the set goes back to it**: every piece to its mark, the lens on, the camera tilted as it was.
- **Record a take.** Press Record, walk an actor across the slate or pan the camera, press Stop, and the move plays back in a loop on the set and on the monitor. An actor carried through a take keeps to the floor when it plays back, turns to face the way it is going, and its legs and arms swing in step with the ground it covers. Record again and what is in the take plays while you carry another piece, so the actor's walk goes down first and the camera's move is laid over it. A take runs up to twelve seconds, and it is kept with the board.
- **You leave holding something.** Back in the browser, save the board as a picture, the shot list as a spreadsheet, and the take as a video animatic filmed through the monitor.
- **It is all still there next time.** Where each piece stands and the whole board are kept in the browser, and the set is parked on a room anchor. After a reload the panel says what came back. Whether the set lands on the same spot after the browser is closed and opened again has been logged on the headset once and not yet checked by eye.
- **An asset library of 51 assets**, on six shelves. Cast: four actors, the eyeline ball, a horse and a wolf. Creatures: dragon, troll, dinosaur, robot, alien, ghost, spider, kraken. Vehicles: car, van, motorbike, truck, pickup, bus, helicopter, boat, flying saucer, bicycle, tank. Effects: fire, smoke, water, explosion, rain, sparks, snow, fog, dust, magic, embers. Set: wall, lamp post, tree, house front, fence, stairs, door, pillar. Props: crate, barrier, bench, cone, dumpster, rock. One pinch adds an item where there is room for it and another puts it back; Remove takes off the piece you last picked up, and Clear set empties the slate. Everything moves, turns and records like any other piece, and the lens sees it. Each frame remembers what was on the set and how it was dressed. Effects show where an effect sits and how big it reads in the frame; they are not simulations.
- **Search and start from a scene.** A letter keyboard on the panel spells a name and finds a piece by what it is called or by another word for it: UFO finds the flying saucer. The Scenes shelf holds ten blockings to start from, such as Car chase, Dragon attack and Haunted house: one press puts every piece on its mark, dressed, with the camera in place, and all of it can be moved.
- **Everything starts as a wooden maquette.** Like a model maker's set, every vehicle, creature and building comes in bare wood beside the lay figures, and colour is a choice you make.
- **Paint, size, fittings and copies.** Press a piece on the shelf, or pick it up, at any time after it is on the set, and a bar at the foot of the panel, on whatever tab is open, offers eight paints for anything with a body, four sizes for anything at all, and fittings where there are some: a light bar or a roof rack on the car, a box, a flatbed or a tanker on the truck, a horn on the horse. Two hands size a piece: hold it with one hand, close the other on it, and pull your hands apart or push them together. It is gentle on purpose: the first 4 cm of a pull do nothing, so a second hand that only came to help carry changes nothing; hands that start close together count as 20 cm apart, so a few centimetres do not double a piece; no piece is stretched longer than 60 cm, about the width of the slate; and it settles on the kit's own sizes when it comes near one. The size is kept with the piece's paint, and the Size button's first press after a stretch takes the piece back to its own size. Copy puts another of the same on the set, dressed the same, for the pieces a set wants several of: trees, rocks, crates, cones, barriers, cars and wolves, up to three of each.
- **Reach in and pick it up.** Put your fingers on a piece and pinch, or close your hand around it, and it comes with your hand; a controller's grip or trigger does the same. From further away the hand's ray and a pinch still work. Near a piece the ray steps aside, so what your hand is on is what you get.
- **A held actor squirms.** Lifted off the slate a lay figure hangs from your hand, swings with it and kicks, and goes quiet when it is set down. The Stage tab turns that off. The camera never does it.
- **Things rest on things.** Let an actor go above the car and they stand on its roof; a crate lands on the truck. Lift what is underneath and what was on it comes down.
- **Push a car and let go, and it drives on.** A vehicle let go while it is moving rolls on along the way it faces, as a toy car does, slowing until it stops. It stops at the slate's edge, against anything solid, or against an actor in its way, who goes down if it meets them at 1 m/s or more on the real set. Only a hand still moving when it opens is a push: carry a car somewhere, stop and let go, and it stays where you put it. A push recorded in a take plays back the same way, the knock-down included; a push only just fast enough to knock them down may leave them standing when the take plays back. The horse and the wolf stand in a vehicle's way too, and go over on their side when it arrives that fast; pick one up and it is back on its feet.
- **Undo.** One press under Snap frame, or the word, puts the set back as it stood before the last change: a piece that was struck comes back, a paint or a size comes off, a move is put back, a knocked-down actor stands, and the whole set goes back where it stood if it was dragged by its edge. Pressed again it goes further back, through the last 24 changes. It does not touch the board: a frame that was snapped stays snapped.
- **The panel says what the set says.** One block under the title repeats the call sheet where the pressing hand is looking: the next step, each passing note for as long as the sheet keeps it, the take's clock, and what a spoken sentence was heard as with what came of it. A refusal is never only on the sheet above the monitor. The block keeps its height, so the buttons under it do not move when the words change, and the piece the panel is about to change keeps its ring on the set.
- **What cannot be taken back takes two presses.** New board, Reset stage and Clear (a take) say what they will cost on the first press and act on the second, within four seconds.
- **Pieces answer each other, by rule.** Rain or water over a fire puts it out and it catches again when the water moves away. A vehicle or a creature driven into an actor knocks them down, and they lie there until a hand stands them up or the take starts again. A vehicle counts as driven when it is going the way it points, or straight back; slid sideways or carried across the set, it knocks nobody down. These are rules read off where things stand. They are not physics, and a take plays them back the same way every time.
- **The library's own reader.** When the set designer cannot be reached, a sentence goes to a reader that ships with the app and knows the library's names: "a red dragon, two actors and a burning car" builds just that. The listening runs on the headset, from a small speech model that ships with the app, so no sound you make leaves it; the model downloads once, the first time you press Talk. This reader can only name what is in the library. Whatever it names that is not there is said back, not invented. It also knows directing words: "a Mexican standoff after a car chase" puts three actors on their marks, each aiming at the next, with two crashed cars behind them. That is a blocking from a short list, built from the same pieces, and every one can be picked up and moved.
- **Say the controls too.** The same button takes the panel's own controls in words: "eighty five", "wider", "snap", "record", "stop", "play it back", "drone", "camera b", "camera on the dragon", "closer", "higher", "paint the car red", "make it bigger", "remove it". "Undo" and "bring it back" take the last change back. Each works the same switch the panel's button works, so a lens that was said and a lens that was pinched are the same thing. A sentence can carry two things: "paint the car red and snap" does both, in that order, and "the cars" or "everyone" changes every one of them. The reader is strict about what counts as a control: a sentence that asks for anything else as well is left whole for the set, so "put a dragon in front of the camera" still adds a dragon. With Keep listening on, on the Stage tab, the microphone opens again after each sentence, so a whole setup can be said after one pinch: a set, where the camera looks, the lens, snap. A quiet spell ends it. Nothing is repainted, resized or struck by word while a take is running. Moving a piece by word ("move the car closer", "turn the dragon to face the camera") goes to the set designer; without it, the sheet says that moving is by hand.
- **Two cameras and a cut.** Add a second camera from the Shoot tab. One is live: its picture is on the monitor and its frame lines are on the floor. Press the other and that is a cut, each camera keeps its own lens, and a take that is recording keeps the cuts and plays them back.
- **Every actor has a colour.** On a set each performer's marks are spike tape in their own colour so nobody walks to the wrong one. Here actor A is orange, B blue, C pink and D green, on the mark under them, on their tag and on their eyeline on the panel. The mark stays on the floor while you carry them, so you can see where they came from.
- **Step inside.** The set comes off the table and grows to full size around you in about a second, into a sky and a ground in the set's own light, with your room still showing faintly through so you know where the furniture is. You stand at the lens, looking down it, the actors are their real height on your real floor, and the tennis ball hangs 3.3 m up where the creature's eyes will be. A take plays around you at that size. Walk it without getting up: point at the floor and a ring shows where you would stand, and a pinch takes you there; or say "take me to the dragon" and you stand a step in front of it, facing it. "Step out" puts the set back on the table. (The arrival and the world around it are checked in the emulator, not yet on the headset.)
- **Actors have poses and looks.** Blocking, neutral, arms up, running, aiming with a wooden prop pistol in the hand, or crouched low to hide; wood, or a costume colour with hair, so two stand-ins read apart in a wide. These are stand-ins: Setpiece does not scan or depict real people.
- **Shot briefs.** Nine shots a director asks for: wide, low-angle hero, high angle, close-up, two-shot, over the shoulder, rule of thirds, creature reveal, aerial. Snap, and the frame is checked against the definition of that shot from where the pieces stand. It is stamped PRINT, or the sheet says what is off in the crew's units, such as "Lens is only 0.2 m below A. Go lower."
- **One line of guidance** above the monitor, repeated on the panel: land the set, say the shot, block it, snap. Each step ends on the thing it asked for, never on a timer, it marks the piece or the button it is asking for, and it goes away when the board is full. During a take it is the take's clock.
